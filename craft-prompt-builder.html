Working with Claude

A 10-minute primer for staff: what Claude is, how a chat actually works, and how to get genuinely useful things out of it.

1What Claude is (and isn't)

Claude is an AI you talk to by typing. It can draft, explain, translate, summarise, plan, and, the part we use most, build small interactive classroom apps from a written description. It responds to what you write the way a very capable, very fast student teacher would: eager, skilled, and entirely dependent on your instructions.

What it isn't: a search engine, a database of true facts, or a mind-reader. It writes what is plausible, and plausible is usually right but sometimes wrong. You stay the subject expert; Claude does the labour.

2The chat is the memory

Inside one chat, Claude remembers everything said so far. That's why you can say "make the buttons bigger" without repeating the whole task: it knows which buttons, because the whole conversation is right there.

A new chat starts fresh. This cuts both ways: start a new chat for a new job (clean slate, no baggage), but stay in the same chat to improve something, because everything you've already fixed together stays fixed.

The most common beginner stumble: asking for changes and thinking nothing happened. Usually the app panel updated quietly, so compare against what you asked for rather than your memory of the old version. And nothing is ever lost: if a change makes things worse, just say "go back to how it was before" and Claude will restore it. If Claude only talked about the changes, reply "please update the app with those changes."

3How to ask: specific beats polite

Claude can't see your classroom. Tell it who the thing is for, what it should achieve, and what must be true. You never need to be brief, and you never need to be formal; you need to be concrete.

Vague"Can you make a maths game for my class?"
Specific"Build a self-checking practice game for Year 2 on number bonds to 10, three difficulty levels, big friendly buttons, works on an iPad."
Starting over (slow)"That's not right." (new chat, reworded prompt, hope)
Iterating (fast)"Good start. Make the numbers bigger, add sound-free celebration animations, and the timer is stressful, remove it."

The second column is the whole skill. Expect two or three rounds of "change this, change that" as completely normal: that's not failure, that's the workflow.

4Classroom apps (artifacts)

When you ask Claude to build something interactive, it appears in a panel beside the chat, ready to use immediately. It updates in place as you request changes, keeps every version so nothing is ever lost, and can be published as a link you share with students or colleagues.

Two things to remember about sharing: a published link is a snapshot, so after making changes you need to publish again for the link to update. And apps built this way work completely on their own: no logins, no internet needed, nothing saved, which is exactly why they're safe to hand to students.

The easiest way to write a great first request is the CRAFT Classroom App Builder: answer five tap-through questions and it writes the prompt for you, tuned to your year level, subject and our curriculum.

5What to expect: the good and the wobbly

Reliably excellent: drafting and rewording, differentiating a task into tiers, building interactive practice apps, generating variations, adjusting reading levels, formatting and structure.

Needs your expert eye: factual content, especially specialist and senior material. Claude's questions and answers are mostly accurate but not always, and it delivers mistakes with the same confidence as correct answers. Review content like the subject expert you are, every time. Apps made through the CRAFT builder include a teacher review screen for exactly this.

The five golden rules

  1. Same chat to improve, new chat for a new job.
  2. Be specific: who it's for, what it should achieve, what must be true.
  3. Iterate, don't restart: say exactly what to change; two or three rounds is normal.
  4. Check the content like the expert you are: confident and correct are not the same thing.
  5. Never paste anything student-identifying: no names, photos, assessment data or reports. Describe the class ("Year 4, mixed ability"), never the child.
